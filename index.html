Indigenous Roots

Long before European arrival, Indigenous nations across North America practiced seasonal harvest ceremonies and living traditions of daily gratitude.

Pre-1500s
Pre-1500s

Wampanoag Seasonal Gratitude Festivals

Wampanoag Nation (Southeastern Massachusetts & Eastern Rhode Island)

For thousands of years prior to European contact, the Wampanoag people held multiple annual ceremonies—including the Green Corn Dance and autumn harvest festivals—centered on giving thanks to the Creator, sharing food, and maintaining balance with the natural world.

Pre-1500s

The Haudenosaunee Thanksgiving Address (Ohén:ton Karihwatéhkwen)

Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy

Meaning "the words that come before all else," the Haudenosaunee practiced a living tradition dating back prior to the 1500s of opening and closing gatherings with a comprehensive expression of gratitude for all elements of creation—from the earth and waters to the plants, animals, and sky.

Pre-1500s

Continental Harvest & Stewardship Traditions

Indigenous Nations across North America

Across the continent before European contact, agricultural and foraging societies—including the Cherokee, Creek, and Pueblos—celebrated seasonal milestones like the Green Corn Ceremony, pairing community feasting and diplomatic renewal with reverence for the harvest.

16th Century

Early European explorers and settlers held formal religious services of thanksgiving upon arriving in North America, decades before the Pilgrims landed at Plymouth.

1500s
1565

St. Augustine Mass & Shared Meal

Spanish Settlers (led by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés) & Timucua People

On September 8, 1565, upon landing in present-day St. Augustine, Florida, Spanish colonists celebrated a Mass of Thanksgiving and shared a communal meal of salted pork, garbanzo beans, and local foods with the indigenous Timucua people—the earliest recorded thanksgiving observance in the continental United States.

1578

Frobisher’s Arctic Service in Present-Day Nunavut

Martin Frobisher

In 1578, English explorer Martin Frobisher and his crew held a formal religious service of thanksgiving in Frobisher Bay (in modern-day Nunavut, Canada) to give thanks for surviving a perilous voyage across the Atlantic—widely recognized as the first European thanksgiving in Canada.

17th Century

English settlements in Virginia and Massachusetts established both secular harvest gatherings and Puritan religious days of thanksgiving that gradually became annual New England customs.

1600s
1619

The Virginia Charter Service at Berkeley Hundred

Captain John Woodlief

On December 4, 1619, thirty-eight English settlers arrived at Berkeley Hundred along Virginia’s James River under a London Company charter decreeing that the day of their arrival be "yearly and perpetually kept holy as a day of thanksgiving to Almighty God."

1621

The Three-Day Plymouth Harvest Feast

Pilgrims (Edward Winslow, William Bradford) & Wampanoag Leader Massasoit (Ousamequin)

Following their first successful harvest in autumn 1621 (between September and November), roughly 50 surviving English colonists at Plymouth held a three-day celebration. Massasoit and about 90 Wampanoag men joined them, contributing five deer to a meal of wild fowl, venison, corn, and shellfish. Contemporaries viewed it as a secular harvest festival and diplomatic alliance rather than a religious thanksgiving.

1623

Plymouth's First Formal Day of Thanksgiving

Governor William Bradford

In July 1623, after a severe drought threatened Plymouth’s crops, colonists held a solemn day of fasting and prayer. When rain arrived shortly after, Bradford proclaimed an official religious day of thanksgiving and prayer—the true precursor to New England's Puritan thanksgiving tradition.

1660

Annual Autumn Observances in New England

New England Colonies

Beginning in the 1660s, scattered colonial days of fasting and thanksgiving gradually stabilized into a regular late-autumn harvest tradition across Massachusetts, Connecticut, and neighboring colonies.

18th Century

During the American Revolution and the founding of the United States, Thanksgiving expanded from a regional New England custom into a federally proclaimed observance.

1700s
1777

First Continental Thanksgiving

Continental Congress

Following the American victory at the Battle of Saratoga during the Revolutionary War, the Continental Congress proclaimed December 18, 1777, as the first thanksgiving observed simultaneously across all 13 states.

1789

George Washington’s Proclamation

President George Washington

At the request of Congress, Washington issued the first presidential Thanksgiving proclamation under the new U.S. Constitution on October 3, 1789, designating Thursday, November 26, 1789, as a day of public thanksgiving and prayer.

19th Century

Writers, magazine editors, and wartime leaders transformed Thanksgiving into an annual national holiday in both the United States and Canada while cementing the Pilgrim origin story.

1800s
1815

Presidential Debate Over Federal Proclamations

Thomas Jefferson & James Madison

Between 1801 and 1809, President Thomas Jefferson declined to issue Thanksgiving proclamations, viewing them as a violation of the separation of church and state. President James Madison revived the practice during the War of 1812, proclaiming a national day of thanksgiving in 1815.

1827

Sarah Josepha Hale’s 36-Year Campaign

Sarah Josepha Hale

Starting in 1827 and continuing until 1863, magazine editor (Godey’s Lady’s Book) and author Sarah Josepha Hale wrote decades of editorials and personal letters to five U.S. presidents, lobbying for a fixed, nationwide Thanksgiving Day to unite the country.

1841

Coining the "First Thanksgiving"

Rev. Alexander Young

In 1841, while publishing primary Pilgrim accounts in Chronicles of the Pilgrim Fathers, historian Alexander Young added a footnote calling the 1621 Plymouth feast "the first Thanksgiving"—sparking the modern origin myth.

1863

Abraham Lincoln’s Proclamation

President Abraham Lincoln

Amid the American Civil War and months after the Union victory at Gettysburg, Lincoln heeded Hale’s appeal on October 3, 1863, proclaiming the last Thursday of November (November 26, 1863) as a national day of "Thanksgiving and Praise" to promote healing and unity.

1876

Thanksgiving Football Begins

Yale vs. Princeton

On November 30, 1876, the Intercollegiate Football Association held its first Thanksgiving Day championship game between Yale and Princeton, launching an enduring American sports tradition later adopted by the NFL.

1879

Canadian National Thanksgiving Established

Parliament of Canada

Following its first civic post-Confederation Thanksgiving on April 5, 1872 (celebrating the Prince of Wales's recovery from illness), Canada's Parliament officially established Thanksgiving as an annual national holiday on November 6, 1879.

20th Century

Federal laws locked in the holiday's modern dates, commercial parades became staples, and Indigenous activists launched the National Day of Mourning to challenge historical myths.

1900s
1924

First Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade

R.H. Macy & Co.

Following Gimbels' 1920 parade in Philadelphia, Macy’s hosted its inaugural parade in New York City on November 27, 1924—featuring live Central Park Zoo animals and kicking off the holiday shopping season.

1941

"Franksgiving" & Congressional Legislation

President Franklin D. Roosevelt & U.S. Congress

Because November 1939 had five Thursdays, FDR moved Thanksgiving one week earlier to lengthen the Great Depression-era Christmas shopping season, sparking fierce political backlash ("Franksgiving") and split state observances between 1939 and 1941. On December 26, 1941, Congress passed a joint resolution fixing Thanksgiving permanently on the fourth Thursday of November.

1957

Canada Fixes Thanksgiving in October

Parliament of Canada

After decades of shifting dates—and to prevent Thanksgiving from colliding with Remembrance Day on November 11—Canada’s Parliament proclaimed on January 31, 1957, that Thanksgiving would be observed permanently on the second Monday of October.

1970

First National Day of Mourning

Wamsutta (Frank) James & United American Indians of New England (UAINE)

After organizers of Plymouth's 350th anniversary censored Wampanoag leader Frank James’s speech detailing colonial violence and land dispossession, Indigenous activists gathered on Cole's Hill in Plymouth on November 26, 1970. They established the annual National Day of Mourning on Thanksgiving Day to honor Indigenous ancestors and protest ongoing injustice.

1989

Official White House Turkey Pardon

President George H. W. Bush

Building on informal gestures dating back to Abraham Lincoln and John F. Kennedy in 1963, President George H. W. Bush formalized the annual White House presidential turkey pardon in November 1989.

Today

Modern Thanksgiving blends family gatherings, multicultural tables, and community service with ongoing reflection on historical accuracy, Indigenous sovereignty, and contemporary traditions.

2000s
2000

Reclaiming Indigenous Foodways & Historical Narratives

Indigenous Chefs, Educators & Tribal Nations

Since the early 2000s, schools, museums, and Indigenous culinary leaders have worked to replace oversimplified Pilgrim myths with historically accurate perspectives—highlighting Indigenous land stewardship, pre-colonial foodways, and continued observance of the National Day of Mourning.

2007

Rise of "Friendsgiving" & Evolving Gatherings

Modern Communities & Chosen Families

Coined in popular culture around 2007, "Friendsgiving" has grown into a widespread modern ritual where friends, neighbors, and chosen families gather before or on Thanksgiving Day—reflecting changing household dynamics and blending classic dishes with diverse regional and immigrant cuisines.

2012

GivingTuesday & Community Volunteerism

Nonprofits, Food Banks & Volunteers

Launched in November 2012 as a philanthropic response to the commercial rush of Black Friday and Cyber Monday, GivingTuesday has reinforced modern Thanksgiving's focus on civic generosity, food drives, and community meal programs.

2026

A Multi-Faceted North American Holiday

Families & Communities Across the U.S. and Canada

Observed on the second Monday of October in Canada and the fourth Thursday of November in the United States, Thanksgiving today stands as the busiest travel period of the year—serving simultaneously as a secular celebration of gratitude, a showcase for televised parades and sports, and a moment of solemn historical reflection.
