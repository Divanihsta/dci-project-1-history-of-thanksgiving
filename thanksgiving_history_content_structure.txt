================================================================================
- .century-title: 16th Century
- .century-description: Early European explorers and settlers held formal religious services of thanksgiving upon arriving in North America, decades before the Pilgrims landed at Plymouth.
- .century-number: 16

[EVENT SLIDE 1 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1565
- .event-slide-title: St. Augustine Mass & Shared Meal
- .event-slide-group: Spanish Settlers (led by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés) & Timucua People
- .event-slide-description: On September 8, 1565, upon landing in present-day St. Augustine, Florida, Spanish colonists celebrated a Mass of Thanksgiving and shared a communal meal of salted pork, garbanzo beans, and local foods with the indigenous Timucua people—the earliest recorded thanksgiving observance in the continental United States.

[EVENT SLIDE 2 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1578
- .event-slide-title: Frobisher’s Arctic Service in Present-Day Nunavut
- .event-slide-group: Martin Frobisher
- .event-slide-description: In 1578, English explorer Martin Frobisher and his crew held a formal religious service of thanksgiving in Frobisher Bay (in modern-day Nunavut, Canada) to give thanks for surviving a perilous voyage across the Atlantic—widely recognized as the first European thanksgiving in Canada.

MAIN -> SECTION 3 (.century#17th-century)
================================================================================
- .century-title: 17th Century
- .century-description: English settlements in Virginia and Massachusetts established both secular harvest gatherings and Puritan religious days of thanksgiving that gradually became annual New England customs.
- .century-number: 17

[EVENT SLIDE 1 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1619
- .event-slide-title: The Virginia Charter Service at Berkeley Hundred
- .event-slide-group: Captain John Woodlief
- .event-slide-description: On December 4, 1619, thirty-eight English settlers arrived at Berkeley Hundred along Virginia’s James River under a London Company charter decreeing that the day of their arrival be "yearly and perpetually kept holy as a day of thanksgiving to Almighty God."

[EVENT SLIDE 2 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1621
- .event-slide-title: The Three-Day Plymouth Harvest Feast
- .event-slide-group: Pilgrims (Edward Winslow, William Bradford) & Wampanoag Leader Massasoit (Ousamequin)
- .event-slide-description: Following their first successful harvest in autumn 1621 (between September and November), roughly 50 surviving English colonists at Plymouth held a three-day celebration. Massasoit and about 90 Wampanoag men joined them, contributing five deer to a meal of wild fowl, venison, corn, and shellfish. Contemporaries viewed it as a secular harvest festival and diplomatic alliance rather than a religious thanksgiving.

[EVENT SLIDE 3 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1623
- .event-slide-title: Plymouth's First Formal Day of Thanksgiving
- .event-slide-group: Governor William Bradford
- .event-slide-description: In July 1623, after a severe drought threatened Plymouth’s crops, colonists held a solemn day of fasting and prayer. When rain arrived shortly after, Bradford proclaimed an official religious day of thanksgiving and prayer—the true precursor to New England's Puritan thanksgiving tradition.

[EVENT SLIDE 4 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1660
- .event-slide-title: Annual Autumn Observances in New England
- .event-slide-group: New England Colonies
- .event-slide-description: Beginning in the 1660s, scattered colonial days of fasting and thanksgiving gradually stabilized into a regular late-autumn harvest tradition across Massachusetts, Connecticut, and neighboring colonies.

MAIN -> SECTION 4 (.century#18th-century)
================================================================================
- .century-title: 18th Century
- .century-description: During the American Revolution and the founding of the United States, Thanksgiving expanded from a regional New England custom into a federally proclaimed observance.
- .century-number: 18

[EVENT SLIDE 1 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1777
- .event-slide-title: First Continental Thanksgiving
- .event-slide-group: Continental Congress
- .event-slide-description: Following the American victory at the Battle of Saratoga during the Revolutionary War, the Continental Congress proclaimed December 18, 1777, as the first thanksgiving observed simultaneously across all 13 states.

[EVENT SLIDE 2 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1789
- .event-slide-title: George Washington’s Proclamation
- .event-slide-group: President George Washington
- .event-slide-description: At the request of Congress, Washington issued the first presidential Thanksgiving proclamation under the new U.S. Constitution on October 3, 1789, designating Thursday, November 26, 1789, as a day of public thanksgiving and prayer.

MAIN -> SECTION 5 (.century#19th-century)
================================================================================
- .century-title: 19th Century
- .century-description: Writers, magazine editors, and wartime leaders transformed Thanksgiving into an annual national holiday in both the United States and Canada while cementing the Pilgrim origin story.
- .century-number: 19

[EVENT SLIDE 1 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1815
- .event-slide-title: Presidential Debate Over Federal Proclamations
- .event-slide-group: Thomas Jefferson & James Madison
- .event-slide-description: Between 1801 and 1809, President Thomas Jefferson declined to issue Thanksgiving proclamations, viewing them as a violation of the separation of church and state. President James Madison revived the practice during the War of 1812, proclaiming a national day of thanksgiving in 1815.

[EVENT SLIDE 2 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1827
- .event-slide-title: Sarah Josepha Hale’s 36-Year Campaign
- .event-slide-group: Sarah Josepha Hale
- .event-slide-description: Starting in 1827 and continuing until 1863, magazine editor (Godey’s Lady’s Book) and author Sarah Josepha Hale wrote decades of editorials and personal letters to five U.S. presidents, lobbying for a fixed, nationwide Thanksgiving Day to unite the country.

[EVENT SLIDE 3 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1841
- .event-slide-title: Coining the "First Thanksgiving"
- .event-slide-group: Rev. Alexander Young
- .event-slide-description: In 1841, while publishing primary Pilgrim accounts in Chronicles of the Pilgrim Fathers, historian Alexander Young added a footnote calling the 1621 Plymouth feast "the first Thanksgiving"—sparking the modern origin myth.

[EVENT SLIDE 4 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1863
- .event-slide-title: Abraham Lincoln’s Proclamation
- .event-slide-group: President Abraham Lincoln
- .event-slide-description: Amid the American Civil War and months after the Union victory at Gettysburg, Lincoln heeded Hale’s appeal on October 3, 1863, proclaiming the last Thursday of November (November 26, 1863) as a national day of "Thanksgiving and Praise" to promote healing and unity.

[EVENT SLIDE 5 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1876
- .event-slide-title: Thanksgiving Football Begins
- .event-slide-group: Yale vs. Princeton
- .event-slide-description: On November 30, 1876, the Intercollegiate Football Association held its first Thanksgiving Day championship game between Yale and Princeton, launching an enduring American sports tradition later adopted by the NFL.

[EVENT SLIDE 6 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1879
- .event-slide-title: Canadian National Thanksgiving Established
- .event-slide-group: Parliament of Canada
- .event-slide-description: Following its first civic post-Confederation Thanksgiving on April 5, 1872 (celebrating the Prince of Wales's recovery from illness), Canada's Parliament officially established Thanksgiving as an annual national holiday on November 6, 1879.

MAIN -> SECTION 6 (.century#20th-century)
================================================================================
- .century-title: 20th Century
- .century-description: Federal laws locked in the holiday's modern dates, commercial parades became staples, and Indigenous activists launched the National Day of Mourning to challenge historical myths.
- .century-number: 20

[EVENT SLIDE 1 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1924
- .event-slide-title: First Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade
- .event-slide-group: R.H. Macy & Co.
- .event-slide-description: Following Gimbels' 1920 parade in Philadelphia, Macy’s hosted its inaugural parade in New York City on November 27, 1924—featuring live Central Park Zoo animals and kicking off the holiday shopping season.

[EVENT SLIDE 2 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1941
- .event-slide-title: "Franksgiving" & Congressional Legislation
- .event-slide-group: President Franklin D. Roosevelt & U.S. Congress
- .event-slide-description: Because November 1939 had five Thursdays, FDR moved Thanksgiving one week earlier to lengthen the Great Depression-era Christmas shopping season, sparking fierce political backlash ("Franksgiving") and split state observances between 1939 and 1941. On December 26, 1941, Congress passed a joint resolution fixing Thanksgiving permanently on the fourth Thursday of November.

[EVENT SLIDE 3 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1957
- .event-slide-title: Canada Fixes Thanksgiving in October
- .event-slide-group: Parliament of Canada
- .event-slide-description: After decades of shifting dates—and to prevent Thanksgiving from colliding with Remembrance Day on November 11—Canada’s Parliament proclaimed on January 31, 1957, that Thanksgiving would be observed permanently on the second Monday of October.

[EVENT SLIDE 4 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1970
- .event-slide-title: First National Day of Mourning
- .event-slide-group: Wamsutta (Frank) James & United American Indians of New England (UAINE)
- .event-slide-description: After organizers of Plymouth's 350th anniversary censored Wampanoag leader Frank James’s speech detailing colonial violence and land dispossession, Indigenous activists gathered on Cole's Hill in Plymouth on November 26, 1970. They established the annual National Day of Mourning on Thanksgiving Day to honor Indigenous ancestors and protest ongoing injustice.

[EVENT SLIDE 5 (.event-slide)]
- .event-slide-badge: 1989
- .event-slide-title: Official White House Turkey Pardon
- .event-slide-group: President George H. W. Bush
- .event-slide-description: Building on informal gestures dating back to Abraham Lincoln and John F. Kennedy in 1963, President George H. W. Bush formalized the annual White House presidential turkey pardon in November 1989.
